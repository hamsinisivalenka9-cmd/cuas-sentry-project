=========================================================
WHY THIS STAGE EXISTS
=========================================================

Stage 4 produced a complete, documented database design -- but a design
document reviewing itself is a weak check. The author of a design is the
person least likely to notice their own blind spots, because every
assumption already feels justified to the person who made it. This stage
exists to apply a different kind of scrutiny: read the design the way
someone who did not write it, and does not trust it, would -- looking
specifically for the failure modes that a design can have even when every
individual sentence in it sounds reasonable. A specific, twenty-five-item
checklist was used (unique keys, foreign keys to non-unique columns,
loading-order cycles, hidden many-to-many relationships, unit ambiguity,
timestamp precision, and more) precisely so the review could not quietly
narrow itself to only the areas the reviewer already felt confident about.

This could not safely be skipped because a database schema is one of the
most expensive things to fix after real data has been loaded through it.
A foreign-key ordering problem, a units mismatch, or a silently-wrong
assumption about which field is safe to treat as unique are all the kind
of defect that either blocks the very first load attempt or, worse,
loads successfully and produces subtly wrong answers that are not
noticed until much later. Catching these now, against documentation, costs
a careful re-read. Catching them after schema.sql exists and data is
loaded costs a migration.

=========================================================
WHAT WE DISCOVERED
=========================================================

- The persistent fused-track identity tables (`cv_tracks`, `fused_tracks`)
  are derived from the *distinct values* found inside their own child
  tables' source data, not from a separate protobuf structure -- and nothing
  in the original Stage 4 design explicitly stated that this means those
  identity tables must be fully populated by a complete, upfront scan
  before any dependent row is inserted, not discovered incrementally.
  Why it matters: a natural, single-pass streaming implementation --
  process each message and insert its rows as they're decoded -- would hit
  a foreign-key failure on the very first message, because a track's
  first-ever appearance in its own state-history table would be inserted
  before that track's identity row exists. This is exactly the kind of
  problem that is invisible on paper and only shows up the moment someone
  tries to actually load data with foreign-key enforcement turned on.

- `cv_track_states.last_associated_detection_id` was relying on an
  implicit fact rather than an explicit one: the design already correctly
  excluded 4 known-orphaned references from the derived bridge table, but
  never explicitly said this same raw column must *also* stay
  unenforced, for the same reason.
  Why it matters: a column that looks exactly like a foreign key, sitting
  right next to real foreign keys, is a natural thing for a future
  engineer to "complete" by adding the constraint -- which would then
  break loading for a reason that would have to be rediscovered from
  scratch, at implementation time, instead of being flagged here.

- A field that was never checked before being designed around --
  `cv_tracks.tracks[].class_distribution`, a repeated, per-candidate-class
  probability structure -- turned out to be fully populated (all 9,496
  track rows carry exactly 4 candidate classifications each). Its
  candidates, however, are only "bird," "air vehicle," and "unknown" --
  never "drone" or "airplane," even as a low-probability alternative.
  Why it matters: this is exactly the kind of investigation a real review
  has to perform rather than skip -- an unchecked omission next to an
  already-flagged, high-stakes open question (the project's Drone/Airplane
  target) is precisely where a review needs to look hardest. In this
  case, the check reinforced the original conclusion rather than
  overturning it, which is itself a useful, evidence-backed outcome, not
  a wasted check.

- A second, previously-unexamined field, `track_states.object_class_
  confidence`, was found fully populated across all 11,630 fused-track
  state rows (with real, varying values, not a constant) -- and had not
  been included as a column in the original Stage 4 design at all.
  Why it matters: a real, populated confidence score sitting directly
  next to a classification the project has already flagged as ambiguous
  is exactly the kind of evidence an analyst investigating that ambiguity
  would want available -- omitting it without ever checking whether it
  existed was a genuine gap, now closed.

- Persistent fused tracks do not always keep one classification for their
  entire lifetime: 18 of the 103 persistent fused tracks (about 1 in 6)
  show more than one distinct classification value across their own
  history. Looking at the actual sequences shows two different real
  patterns -- some are a single-frame blip surrounded by an otherwise
  stable classification, and others are a clean, sustained switch from
  one classification to another partway through the track's life.
  Why it matters: this means "the classification of a fused track" is not
  a simple, given fact the way "the classification of a single detection"
  is -- it has to be resolved by an explicit rule, and different
  reasonable rules (first seen, last seen, most common, highest
  confidence, or not resolving to one value at all) would give different
  answers for these 18 tracks. The original Stage 4 design's description
  of the "duration by class" analytical question as a simple one-view,
  one-`GROUP BY` query did not account for this at all.

- Once several radar fields were checked at full scale rather than left
  unverified, `radar_detections` turned out to have far less usable
  signal than its column list implied: `range`, `range_rate`, `snr`,
  `filter_type`, `estimated_width_meters`, and `is_marked` are all
  confirmed constant or empty across every one of the 2,929 radar
  detections in this file. By contrast, `azimuth` and `elevation` are
  genuinely populated, and their actual observed value ranges (matching
  zero to two-pi) gave enough evidence to confirm they are recorded in
  radians, not degrees -- a unit that was never stated in the original
  design at all.
  Why it matters: an unlabeled angle column is a real, specific risk for
  a future analyst to silently compute wrong geometry with; and knowing
  in advance that most of this table's columns carry no real signal in
  this particular file sets honest expectations for what "radar
  analysis" can actually show, rather than an implicit promise the
  column list did not actually back up.

- Nanosecond-precision timestamps, stored as SQLite's `INTEGER` type,
  were confirmed -- by direct arithmetic, not assumption -- to have
  roughly 5.2 times the headroom before any 64-bit overflow risk, given
  the actual timestamp values observed in this file.
  Why it matters: this had already been designed correctly in Stage 4,
  but had never been explicitly checked and stated as checked -- this
  review turned an implicit, reasonable assumption into an explicit,
  confirmed fact, which is a small but real improvement in how much a
  future reader can trust the design without re-deriving it themselves.

=========================================================
ENGINEERING DECISIONS
=========================================================

Decision: Recommend a concrete two-phase loading strategy (envelope and
identity tables fully populated first; everything else second) rather
than recommending any relaxation of foreign-key enforcement.
Reason: explicit instruction, and good practice independent of the
instruction -- CLAUDE.md rule 8 requires foreign-key enforcement to
actually be active, and a solution that works around that requirement
defeats the purpose of having it.
Alternative considered: recommend `PRAGMA defer_foreign_keys = ON`
(a SQLite feature that delays constraint checking until the end of a
transaction) as the primary fix, allowing a simpler single-pass streaming
load.
Why the chosen approach is better: the two-phase strategy is simpler to
reason about and test -- it is a plain, sequential algorithm with no
transaction-scoped subtlety to get wrong. The deferred-constraint
technique was still noted as a valid alternative for an implementer who
strongly prefers single-pass streaming, but was deliberately not made the
primary recommendation.
Future impact: Stage 5B/6's ETL implementation now has an unambiguous,
tested-on-paper loading algorithm to follow, and does not need to
rediscover the two-phase requirement by hitting a foreign-key failure
during development.

Decision: Investigate `class_distribution` and `object_class_confidence`
with real, full-dataset queries before deciding whether they change the
schema, rather than reasoning about them in the abstract.
Reason: explicit instruction, and the only way to actually answer "should
this have been included" honestly -- a decision made without checking
population would just be a different unverified assumption replacing the
first one.
Alternative considered: leave both fields as previously omitted /
unexamined, on the reasoning that the compact-design goal already argued
against adding more columns.
Why the chosen approach is better: the investigation found a real,
populated, directly relevant field (`object_class_confidence`) that had
simply never been looked at -- an outcome that could not have been
predicted without actually checking, and that a "no new columns" policy
applied without checking would have missed permanently.
Future impact: the classification-semantics comparison (see below) is now
grounded in a real confidence signal instead of a hypothetical one, and
the schema carries the evidence needed to support whichever resolution
rule is eventually chosen.

Decision: Define and compare five possible classification-resolution
semantics for "the" class of a fused track, without selecting one.
Reason: explicit instruction not to choose a semantic unless the source
evidence or the assignment's own wording justifies one -- and neither
does. The five options are each defensible under different reasonable
interpretations of "the" class of a track, and the real evidence (18 of
103 tracks showing genuine within-track changes) shows the choice
actually matters, rather than being an academic distinction.
Alternative considered: pick the semantic that seems most standard for
tracking systems (for example, "most frequent class") and document it as
the recommended default.
Why the chosen approach is better: picking one, even as a "default,"
would have been exactly the kind of invented business rule the project
has consistently avoided at every prior stage when the data did not
justify a specific choice -- and doing so here would risk quietly
resolving part of the still-open Drone/Airplane question by fiat instead
of leaving it genuinely open for a human decision.
Future impact: whoever answers CLAUDE.md's duration-by-class question
later has real trade-offs and real evidence in front of them, not a
blank page and not a silently-already-decided answer.

Decision: Remove two indexes identified as redundant with existing
UNIQUE constraints, rather than leaving them for "extra safety."
Reason: SQLite automatically creates an index to enforce a UNIQUE
constraint, and by the standard leftmost-prefix rule for multi-column
indexes, that automatic index already serves any query filtering on just
the first column of the constraint -- a separately declared single-column
index on that same first column adds no additional query capability.
Alternative considered: leave both indexes in place, reasoning that extra
indexes are harmless.
Why the chosen approach is better: they are not entirely harmless -- they
cost extra maintenance during every insert and extra storage, for zero
benefit, and leaving them in place would misrepresent the design as
having made a deliberate indexing choice there when it had actually just
duplicated an existing one.
Future impact: two fewer indexes for the Stage 5B/6 implementation to
create and keep in sync with the constraint list.

INTERVIEW EXPLANATION
=========================================================

"After I finished the database design, I didn't treat it as done -- I
went back through it the way someone trying to poke holes in it would,
using a specific checklist so I couldn't just gravitate toward the parts
I already felt good about. The most useful thing that checklist forced me
to notice was a loading-order problem that would have been completely
invisible on paper: two of my tables are built by scanning for distinct
values inside other tables' own data, not from a separate source field.
That's fine in theory, but it means a normal, single-pass way of loading
the file -- read a message, insert its rows -- would try to insert a
child row referencing an identity that doesn't exist in the parent table
yet, and fail immediately with foreign keys turned on. That's exactly the
kind of thing that looks correct in a design document and then blows up
the first time someone actually tries to load data through it, so I
worked out a concrete two-phase loading order that avoids it without
weakening any constraint, and wrote it down explicitly instead of leaving
it to be rediscovered later. The other thing I'm glad I did was go back
and actually check two fields I'd previously decided not to include,
instead of trusting my earlier reasoning. One of them, a per-track
classification confidence score, turned out to be fully populated and
directly relevant to a question the project already knew was unresolved
-- I added it. The other confirmed that my original decision to leave a
field out was actually correct, which is just as valuable an outcome,
because now it's backed by evidence instead of assumption. And when that
same investigation showed that some tracks in the data genuinely change
classification partway through their history, I made a point of not
quietly picking a rule for how to handle that -- I laid out five
reasonable ways to define 'the' classification of a track, with real
examples from the data showing why they'd give different answers, and
left the actual choice for a human decision, because inventing that rule
myself would have been exactly the kind of unjustified assumption this
whole project has been careful to avoid."

=========================================================
BEGINNER TAKEAWAYS
=========================================================

- A "foreign key" only works if the row it points to already exists in
  the database at the moment the referencing row is inserted -- not
  eventually, not "later in the same file." This project discovered that
  two of its tables are built by extracting a list of unique values from
  data that lives in a *different* table, which means those two tables
  have to be completely finished before anything that points to them can
  be loaded. This is a genuinely common trap in real-world data
  engineering: it is easy to design a correct-looking set of tables and
  relationships and only discover the loading-order problem once you
  actually try to load data through them in the "obvious" order.

- Reviewing your own work is real, valuable engineering work -- it is not
  a formality to get through before the "real" work of writing code
  begins. This stage did not write a single line of implementation code,
  and still found a problem (the loading order) that would have caused a
  real failure, plus a genuinely useful improvement (the confidence
  column) that would otherwise have simply never existed.

- Checking an assumption and confirming it was already correct is not a
  wasted step. Several things in this review turned out to already be
  fine -- but "fine, and now I can prove it" is a meaningfully stronger
  position than "fine, probably" for anyone who has to trust this design
  later without re-deriving it themselves.

- Real-world data is often messier than a single, tidy category can
  capture. This project found that some tracked objects were classified
  one way for a while and then a different way later in the same
  tracking session -- which means a question like "what is this object's
  classification" does not always have one unambiguous answer, and
  picking how to define "the" answer is itself a real decision that
  deserves to be made deliberately, with evidence, rather than by
  accident or by whichever rule happened to be easiest to write first.

- A "redundant index" is extra bookkeeping a database maintains that does
  not actually help answer any question faster, because something else
  already provides the same capability. Finding and removing this kind of
  small inefficiency is a normal, ongoing part of database design review
  -- not a sign that the original design was careless, just a sign that a
  second, careful pass catches things a first pass reasonably misses.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
=========================================================

Before any schema.sql or database existed, this stage put the completed
Stage 4 database design through a skeptical, checklist-driven review --
re-examining every one of its 10 tables and 14 named key decisions, and
gathering new, full-dataset evidence (not assumptions) for every question
the existing project evidence had not already settled. The review found
one genuine gap that would have caused a real implementation failure: the
design never stated that two of its tables, both built from distinct
values found inside other tables' data, must be fully loaded before
anything referencing them, which a naturally-written single-pass loader
would violate under foreign-key enforcement -- a concrete, tested-on-paper
two-phase loading strategy was written to close this gap without weakening
any constraint. The review also investigated two previously-unchecked
protobuf fields relevant to the project's already-unresolved
classification question, finding one worth adding to the schema (a
per-track classification confidence score, now included) and confirming
the other's original omission was already correct. In the course of that
investigation, the review discovered that some persistent tracked objects
genuinely change classification partway through their own history --
meaning "the" classification of a track is itself an open modeling
question, not a given fact, and five possible ways to define it were
compared without picking one, since neither the data nor the assignment's
wording justifies choosing on its own. Two minor redundant indexes were
removed, several radar measurement fields were confirmed to carry no real
signal in this file (with their units, previously unstated, confirmed
from observed value patterns), and one implicit design choice was made
explicit to prevent a future implementer from accidentally breaking it.
Critically, zero of the 10 tables and zero of the 14 previously-approved
key decisions were reversed -- the core architecture held up under
skeptical review, and the two headline unresolved analytical questions
from Stage 4 (the ~81% CV-incorporation target and the Drone/Airplane
duration question) remain exactly as unresolved as before, now with
stronger, more thoroughly checked evidence behind that conclusion rather
than a manufactured answer. The design is now ready for actual
implementation in the next stage.
